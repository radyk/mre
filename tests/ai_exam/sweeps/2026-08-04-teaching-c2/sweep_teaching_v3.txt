    An optimality gap is the reported distance between the best schedule the solver found and the best mathematically possible schedule (a proven lower bound) for the same cost objective — it is not a measure of how good the plan looks or feels, only how far the solver's proof has closed.  [general knowledge — how scheduling works in general, not a fact about this plan]
    A gap of, say, 3% means the solver can prove no solution could cost more than 3% less than the one it found — it does NOT mean the plan is 3% away from perfect in any practical sense, and a large gap does not automatically mean the schedule is bad, only that the proof is loose (this is common with tardiness-weighted objectives, which tend to produce weak bounds).  [synthesis — read from: 3c67fcfd, 47a25482, 70a2f322]
    Read a gap by what it does to your decision, not as a grade: a small gap (under roughly 1-2%) means further solve time won't buy much improvement, while a large or non-shrinking gap over successive solves usually means the model is starved for solve time or the problem structure (many tied-cost alternatives, weak bounds from tardiness terms) is inherently hard to prove tight, not that the schedule itself is necessarily far from good.
