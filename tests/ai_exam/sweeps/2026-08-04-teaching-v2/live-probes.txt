Scheduling problems resist proof of optimality mainly because the search space of feasible sequences and timings grows combinatorially with the number of jobs and machines, so no algorithm can check every possibility in reasonable time — proof of optimality then requires a lower bound that provably matches the best schedule found, not just the schedule itself.  [general knowledge — how scheduling works in general, not a fact about this plan]
Several features common in real plants make that lower bound hard to tighten: sequence-dependent setup times, tardiness or lateness objectives (as opposed to simple makespan), machine-sharing or eligibility constraints, and split or resumable operations each add combinatorial interactions that weaken the relaxations used to bound the true optimum.  [general knowledge — how scheduling works in general, not a fact about this plan]
Tardiness-based objectives in particular tend to give weak lower bounds compared to makespan objectives, because a schedule's tardiness depends on the interaction of due dates and sequencing decisions across the whole horizon, not on any single machine in isolation — so relaxing the problem to bound it loses most of the structure that determines the real answer.
